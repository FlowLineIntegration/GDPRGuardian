# gdpr-legal

### Key takeaways
- Status (24 Oct 2026). EDPB Guidelines 01/2025 on pseudonymisation are still only the v1 draft: consultation closed on 14 Mar 2025 and no final version is listed. The EDPB says it is reassessing its anonymisation and pseudonymisation work after the SRB judgment. The current GDPR applies in full and none of the Omnibus changes is law.
- EDPB Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation were adopted on 8 Jul 2026 and are in consultation until 30 Oct 2026. Identifiability is relative ('may vary from one entity to another'), with a contextual or a simplified approach. Data is anonymous if it meets three criteria: no record isolation, no linkage, no inference. Anonymising is itself processing, it must be documented, and it must be reassessed periodically.
- CJEU C-413/23 P EDPS v SRB (4 Sep 2025). Pseudonymised data are not personal data 'in all cases and for every person' (para 86). They remain personal data for the controller that holds the key (para 76). A recipient with no means reasonably likely to re-identify may hold non-personal data (para 77). Transparency about recipients is judged from the controller's view at the time of collection (paras 102, 112). Opinions 'relate to' their author by their content.
- WP29 Opinion 05/2014 (WP216) is still the baseline. Pseudonymisation is not anonymisation. It sets three risks: singling out, linkability, inference. CNIL gives a directly relevant example: a CV file where only the names are replaced by a number remains pseudonymised.
- Digital Omnibus (GDPR), COM(2025) 837 of 19 Nov 2025. The Commission proposed a relative definition of personal data in Art 4(1), Commission implementing acts on pseudonymisation (new Art 41a), breach notification only above a high-risk threshold within 96h, and Art 88c on legitimate interest for AI. The Council mandate of 21 Oct 2026 keeps the current definition, adds a pseudonymisation provision and supports a longer breach deadline. Parliament has no position yet (committee vote expected early 2027).
- Placeholder output from free text should not be called 'anonymised'. The customer still holds the original, so the data stays personal for them. For a third-party LLM, residual quasi-identifiers (roles, dates, rare events, places, writing style) usually keep re-identification possible. Honest terms: 'pseudonymisation' in reversible mode; 'désidentification / masquage des identifiants directs' and 'réduction du risque' when the mapping is destroyed.
- With an ephemeral mapping that is destroyed, the output is not Art 4(5) pseudonymisation in the strict sense (no additional information is kept) and not anonymisation (quasi-identifiers remain). The defensible claims are data minimisation (Art 5(1)(c)), protection by design (Art 25), a security measure (Art 32) and lower transfer and breach impact. Indexed placeholders are better than hash tokens: hashes of identifiers (phones, emails, NIR) can be brute-forced.
- In reversible mode the mapping is the 'pseudonymisation secret' and is itself personal data. It is the most sensitive asset. Keep it in the customer's 'pseudonymisation domain': ideally the client or local MCP holds it, or the server returns it encrypted and stays stateless. Otherwise keep it in RAM only, with a short TTL, encrypted under an ephemeral key, never logged.
- Roles. Self-hosted MIT software: the project is only a software publisher, with no GDPR processor role. Hosted service: we are a processor (Art 28) for payloads, and a controller for accounts, billing and security logs. The customer stays controller of what it sends to LLMs. The LLM provider is the customer's recipient or processor, and transfers to the US still fall under Chapter V from the exporter's side (SRB para 76).
- AI Act. The encoder NER model is likely an 'AI system' (ML inference); regex, checksum and dictionary parts likely are not. The tool is not prohibited, not high-risk and not GPAI. Art 2(12) exempts open-source releases unless high-risk, Art 5 or Art 50. The Art 50(2) marking duty does not apply to assistive editing or to output that does not substantially alter the input. Art 4 AI literacy applies. AI Omnibus Reg (EU) 2026/1744 is in force since 27 Jul 2026.
- Hosting. CLOUD Act 18 USC 2713 compels US providers to disclose data 'regardless of whether … located within or outside of the United States'. Use an EU-owned host, with no US CDN or WAF terminating TLS on payloads. Offer a SecNumCloud-qualified tier for the public sector and sensitive customers. EU-US DPF is in force but under appeal (C-703/25 P), so avoid depending on it.
- HDS (French health data). The HDS v2 transition ended on 16 May 2026. Storage must be in the EEA, and remote access from outside the EEA must be mapped and disclosed. Whether a stateless SaaS that processes without storing needs certification (activity 5, 'administration et exploitation') is unclear: ask ANS before marketing to health actors.
- Excel (the user's question), legal angle. Spreadsheets are structured datasets, so WP29 singling-out and linkage risks apply directly through quasi-identifier columns (date of birth, postal code, job title). They often hold large HR or customer files, which raises the DPIA case for customers. They carry hidden sheets, comments, author metadata, pivot caches and external links. Handle Excel as 'tabular pseudonymisation', never claim anonymisation, and strip hidden and metadata content.

### Recommendations
- Wording policy. Never write 'anonymisation conforme RGPD' or 'données anonymes'. Use: 'pseudonymisation réversible (session)'; 'désidentification : masquage des identifiants directs' (irreversible mode); 'réduction du risque de ré-identification'. Add a residual-risk disclaimer: quasi-identifiers in free text may remain, and the output usually stays personal data. Consider a disclaimer on the names 'Gardien RGPD' and 'GDPR Guardian' so they do not read as a compliance certification.
- Architecture for legal minimisation. Process payloads in memory only (no disk, no swap, no core dumps, request-scoped buffers). Reversible mode: return the mapping to the caller encrypted, or keep it in the local MCP or SDK, so the server stays stateless. If a server-side session is needed: RAM-only store, TTL of 24h or less, ephemeral key, explicit delete endpoint, no backups.
- Placeholders: indexed tokens like [PERSONNE_1] that carry no information about the original. Use HMAC with an ephemeral key only for consistency within a session, and destroy the key at session end. Never use plain hashes. Never reuse keys across tenants or sessions, so outputs cannot be linked.
- Logging policy (publish it). Log metadata only: tenant ID, timestamp, endpoint, byte and token counts, entity counts per type, country pack, latency, model versions, error codes. Never log payloads, entity values or hashes of values. Scrub exceptions and tracebacks; disable body logging in the reverse proxy, APM and WAF. Keep security logs 6-12 months (CNIL 2021-122). Treat IP addresses as personal data.
- Documents to publish: (1) a DPA based on SCC 2021/915, with Annex I (processing description), Annex II (technical and organisational measures) and Annex III (sub-processors); (2) a public sub-processor list with 30-day notice and right to object (Art 28(2)); (3) a security whitepaper (architecture, no persistence, encryption, isolation, key management, model provenance, pentest summary); (4) a privacy policy (Art 13) for site and accounts; (5) a cookie statement; (6) a ROPA template entry and a DPIA support pack for customers; (7) a detection-limits sheet (per-country coverage, measured recall and precision).
- Processor obligations checklist: Art 28 DPA signed before go-live. Art 30(2) processor register. Art 32 measures (TLS 1.3, tenant isolation, least privilege, MFA admin access, patching, annual pentest). Art 33(2): notify the controller without undue delay, with a contractual target such as 24-48h, plus an internal breach runbook. Art 28(3)(e)-(f) help with data subject requests and DPIAs. Art 28(10): never use payloads for training or analytics.
- Appoint a DPO (or at least a privacy lead). Processing customer payloads that may contain special categories at scale may trigger Art 37(1)(c) for a processor. Run our own voluntary DPIA of the hosted service and publish a summary.
- Hosting: EU-owned provider with an EU DPA and EU-only sub-processors (IaaS, anti-DDoS, email, support desk, monitoring). No US CDN terminating TLS. Offer a premium tier on SecNumCloud-qualified infrastructure. Document a transfer map stating that there are no transfers.
- LLM proxy mode: bring-your-own-key. The customer contracts directly with the LLM provider (its processor and transfer tool: DPF or SCCs). We only relay the pseudonymised payload under the customer's instructions. Do not resell LLM access, which would make the LLM provider our sub-processor and create US transfers.
- Handling data subject requests in a stateless service: state in the DPA and privacy notice that we keep no payloads, so we hold no data to give access to or erase. Forward any request to the customer within 5 working days (Art 28(3)(e)). Invoke Art 11 where relevant. For account and controller data, provide the normal Art 15-22 process.
- Cookie-less website: no non-essential cookies or trackers. Analytics either absent or self-hosted and configured within the CNIL audience-measurement exemption. No third-party fonts or CDNs that leak IP addresses to the US. Then no consent banner is needed under ePrivacy Art 5(3) and Art 82 of the Loi Informatique et Libertés.
- AI Act hygiene: model cards with weights licence and training-data provenance, version pinning, an AI literacy note for staff, and a statement that no generative LLM is used. Avoid realistic fake surrogates (keep [TYPE_N] placeholders) so the product stays clearly outside Art 50.
- Health data: until ANS confirms whether stateless processing falls within the HDS scope, mark health-care producers as unsupported on the online service and steer them to the self-hosted MIT edition. Alternatively host on an HDS v2-certified provider and evaluate our own activity 5 certification. NIR: remind customers that using the NIR is restricted by décret n° 2019-341 (verify) and that our recognition does not authorise processing it.
- Excel decision (user's question): support .xlsx/.csv in scope as 'tabular pseudonymisation'. (a) Convert to Markdown tables, which drops formulas, macros and external links. (b) Strip or flag hidden sheets, hidden rows and columns, comments, document properties (author, company), pivot caches and named ranges. (c) Use column headers (Nom, NIR, Date de naissance) for deterministic column-level detection. (d) Offer deterministic generalisation options for quasi-identifier columns (year only, département only), never labelled as anonymisation. (e) Cap file size and rows to limit the impact of a breach.
- Certification roadmap: ISO 27001 within 12-18 months, then ISO 27701. Consider the EU Cloud Code of Conduct (Art 40) or an Art 42 seal such as Europrivacy. Check NIS2 applicability once headcount or turnover reaches medium size. Add Data Act switching and exit clauses to the terms of service if the online service is paid.

### Risks
- Overclaiming 'anonymisation'. Customers may then believe GDPR no longer applies, skip the DPA or SCCs with US LLM providers, and blame us. This also exposes us to misleading-commercial-practice claims and loss of trust with the CNIL.
- False negatives (missed names, rare identifiers, quasi-identifiers in narrative text) can leak personal data to third-party LLMs. The DPA needs a clear liability allocation, a statement of residual risk and published benchmarks.
- The reversible-mode mapping store is a concentrated re-identification secret. A server-side compromise exposes identifiers plus context, which is a personal data breach. Unauthorised reversal also counts as a breach under the EDPB 01/2025 draft.
- Payload leakage through peripheral systems: reverse-proxy or WAF body logging, APM and error trackers, crash dumps, swap, support tickets with pasted samples, US CDN TLS termination.
- Regulatory moving targets. The final EDPB 01/2025 and 02/2026 texts may tighten or loosen the stance after SRB. The Omnibus may add a pseudonymisation provision and change breach deadlines. Wording and the DPA will need revision in 2027.
- Uncertain HDS scope for stateless processing of French health data. Operating without certification where it is required is a criminal offence risk under the CSP (unverified in this run).
- Excel-specific leaks: hidden sheets, comments, metadata, cached pivot data, embedded objects, cell values in chart caches. Tabular quasi-identifiers allow singling out even after names are masked.
- NER weights trained on data of unclear lawfulness (e.g. scraped corpora) could raise Opinion 28/2024 issues and licence or provenance problems. Document provenance per model.
- The DPF appeal (C-703/25 P) could invalidate US transfers that customers rely on for downstream LLMs. Our tool reduces impact but is not a transfer mechanism.
- Special categories and NIR in payloads mean a higher-risk processor profile. A DPO may be mandatory, and customers will demand a DPIA and audit rights.

### Verifier notes
- WORDING CONFLICT (important): EDPB Guidelines 02/2026 para 40 says controllers 'should not use descriptions like "anonymous", "de-identified" or "de-personalised" if individuals are actually still identifiable'. The recommended label 'désidentification : masquage des identifiants directs' conflicts with this. Use 'masquage des identifiants directs' or 'pseudonymisation' without the word 'désidentification', or always pair it with 'les données restent personnelles'.
- MISSED KEY POINT on processors: EDPB 02/2026 para 15 and Example 3 say that a party processing on behalf of a controller takes the controller's perspective, so the data are personal for it too. Consequences: (a) the hosted GDPR Guardian is a processor whatever the masking quality; (b) an LLM provider acting as the customer's processor (typical API with a DPA, BYOK) receives personal data by definition, whatever its own means. The takeaway's argument about 'residual quasi-identifiers' only matters if the LLM provider is an independent controller. Para 21 adds that data are personal for both sides if an onward transfer to an entity able to re-identify is reasonably likely.
- EDPB 02/2026 points that support existing recommendations and can be cited: para 50 (pseudonyms that can be easily reverse-engineered, e.g. plain hashes, are 'clearly not anonymous'), para 91 (encryption is not anonymisation), para 30 (relevant attackers include foreign intelligence agencies and cybercriminals, which supports the hosting and CLOUD Act reasoning), para 38 (anonymisation needs an Art 6 legal basis and an Art 9(2) exemption; add this to the customer DPIA pack), para 36 (if any record is identifiable, the whole dataset is treated as personal).
- Excel decision, legal support: EDPB 02/2026 reasons on tables throughout (Ex 10 and 12: sex, date of birth, postcode, disease). Para 85 names dimensionality (number of columns) and resolution (full date of birth vs year) as the key risk factors, which directly supports column-level detection and generalisation options. Example 12 shows generalised (k-anonymous-like) tables can still fail No Linkage and No Inference through homogeneity, so the 'never label generalisation as anonymisation' rule is correct and should cite this example.
- C-413/23 P nuances missing from the takeaways: it interprets Regulation 2018/1725 (EUDPR), applied to the GDPR by analogy, and the case was referred back to the General Court.
- Claim 2 date: the guidelines were adopted on 7 July 2026 (per the PDF); 8 July is the news and consultation-start date.
- Omissions: the EDPB published a DPIA template (consultation 14 Apr-9 Jun 2026) and a breach-notification template (10 Jun-5 Aug 2026). Use them for the 'DPIA support pack' and the 'breach runbook'. Also new: Guidelines 1/2026 on scientific research (cited in 02/2026 fn 33) and Guidelines 04/2026 on fines (consultation until 13 Nov 2026).
- AI Act Art 4: artificialintelligenceact.eu marks Art 4(1) as amended by the AI Omnibus and adds new paras 2-3 (Commission, Member States and Board support AI literacy). The duty on providers and deployers seems to remain but may be softened. Check the OJ text of Reg 2026/1744 before writing 'Art 4 AI literacy applies' unchanged. Art 50 applies from 2 Aug 2026.
- HDS: per Legiscope (secondary), since 26 Sept 2026 CSP R.1111-9-1 makes EU/EEA storage a legal requirement, remote access from a third country counts as a transfer, and R.1111-11 requires a public mapping of transfers and remote access. The takeaway should cite these articles; the implementing decree was not identified and should be checked on Légifrance. Legiscope also says a SaaS that administers the system holding health data must examine activity 5, which supports 'ask ANS'.
- Debatable takeaway: 'with an ephemeral mapping destroyed, the output is not Art 4(5) pseudonymisation'. The customer still holds the original text, and that original can serve as the 'additional information'. It is safer to say the output remains personal data for the customer and for any processor, and to avoid a firm legal classification.
- 'Forward DSARs within 5 working days (Art 28(3)(e))': the 5 days is a contractual choice. Art 28(3)(e) only requires assistance, so do not present the deadline as a legal requirement.
- ISO/IEC 27701:2025 is now standalone and can be certified without ISO 27001, so the roadmap order (27001 first, then 27701) is optional, not required.
- The CLOUD Act reaches any provider subject to US jurisdiction, including EU subsidiaries or EU firms with US presence, not just US-owned hosts. FISA 702 is not mentioned; its status after the April 2026 sunset should be checked (not verified here).
